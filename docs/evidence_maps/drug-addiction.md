# Drug Addiction Treatment Harm Reduction Evidence Map

> **Draft Phase 3 research prototype — not a charity recommendation.** This map grades intervention categories. It does not establish a charity's implementation fidelity, marginal cost-effectiveness, or room for more funding.

- **Review question:** For people who use drugs or have substance use disorders in the United States, which voluntary treatments and harm-reduction services reduce death and improve health, and which approaches create avoidable harm?
- **Population/geography:** People who use drugs or meet criteria for substance use disorders in U.S. community, clinical, and justice settings.

## Plain-language summary

This is not a charity recommendation. This map compares intervention categories, not charities. Grades describe the current direction and credibility of evidence; they are not rankings and do not establish marginal cost-effectiveness or room for more funding.

The table deliberately preserves favorable, uncertain, weak, and harmful findings. A `Strong` grade means the intervention category has comparatively credible evidence for specified outcomes—not that every provider is effective or that an additional donation is cost-effective.

## Search and selection method

- **Repositories:** PubMed, Campbell Collaboration, U.S. federal evidence repositories, National Academies, targeted citation chaining
- **Search concepts:** cause area + intervention + systematic review; cause area + randomized evaluation; intervention + harm + United States
- **Included:** Public systematic reviews, meta-analyses, randomized or credible quasi-experimental studies, government evaluations, and major implementation syntheses relevant to U.S. delivery.
- **Excluded:** Testimonials, charity marketing, uncontrolled output counts used as impact evidence, inaccessible claims without traceable citations, and studies with no relevant outcome.
- **Screening:** Single-reviewer Phase 3 prototype with structured extraction; grades requiring Strong or Potentially Harmful receive mandatory second review before publication or recommendation use.
- **Risk of bias:** Design, assignment, baseline balance, attrition, outcome validity, selective reporting, confounding, replication, and applicability assessed narratively under the evidence-grading method.
- **Protocol deviations:** This is a rapid single-reviewer prototype map, not a completed systematic review. Searches were targeted rather than exhaustive and no dual screening or formal meta-analysis was performed.
- **Missing-literature risks:** Publication bias, incomplete grey-literature capture, English-language bias, and fast-changing policy or drug-market contexts.

## Outcome metrics

### D-M1: All-cause and overdose mortality

- **Unit:** deaths per 1,000 person-years
- **Decision importance:** primary
- **Interpretation:** Track induction, retention, and post-discontinuation periods.

### D-M2: Treatment retention and use outcomes

- **Unit:** retention, abstinent samples, use days
- **Decision importance:** secondary
- **Interpretation:** Do not require abstinence as the only valued outcome.

### D-M3: Infectious and acute harms

- **Unit:** HIV/HCV incidence, overdoses reversed, hospital use
- **Decision importance:** primary
- **Interpretation:** Measure reach and sterile-equipment coverage.

## Intervention details

### D-I1: Methadone or buprenorphine treatment for opioid use disorder

**Evidence grade: Strong (high confidence).** Strong: based on the cited evidence, with no automatic transfer of this grade to a charity.

- **Definition and theory of change:** Provide opioid agonist medication at adequate dose with retention-oriented clinical care.
- **Target population and setting:** U.S. populations eligible for the intervention, with subgroup and local-system applicability checked before use.
- **Comparator:** Usual services, no intervention, or the most credible alternative reported by included studies.
- **Outcomes and timeframe:** All-cause and overdose mortality, retention, illicit opioid use, and health over treatment and post-treatment periods.
- **Evidence summary/effect range:** Large meta-analyses show mortality is markedly lower in treatment than out of treatment; risk rises after cessation.
- **Key studies:** [DRG-01](#drg-01), [DRG-02](#drg-02)
- **Causal strength and bias:** Grade reflects the strongest applicable causal evidence and downgrades for bias, inconsistency, indirectness, or implementation variation.
- **U.S. applicability:** Direct U.S. evidence is prioritized; transfer across populations and delivery systems remains an explicit uncertainty.
- **Cost/resource evidence:** Not standardized in Phase 3. Record direct program cost, participant time, public-system use, price year, and perspective in Phase 4.
- **Implementation and fidelity:** Low-barrier access, medication choice, adequate dose, continuity, respectful care, and overdose prevention at transitions.
- **Scale, equity, and distribution:** Track reach, take-up, exclusion, racial/ethnic disparities, disability access, rural access, and burdens on participants—not only average effects.
- **Harms:** Induction risk, forced taper, punitive discharge, and interrupted access can increase mortality.
- **Recommended use:** People with OUD who choose medication, including during pregnancy and justice-system transitions with specialist guidance.
- **Do not use as:** Do not use detoxification alone as equivalent treatment or force discontinuation after arbitrary duration.
- **Primary metrics:** D-M1, D-M2
- **Open questions:** Fentanyl-era induction and retention; Best models for rural and polysubstance use

### D-I2: Community overdose education and naloxone distribution

**Evidence grade: Strong (high confidence).** Strong: based on the cited evidence, with no automatic transfer of this grade to a charity.

- **Definition and theory of change:** Place naloxone and practical overdose response training with people likely to witness an overdose.
- **Target population and setting:** U.S. populations eligible for the intervention, with subgroup and local-system applicability checked before use.
- **Comparator:** Usual services, no intervention, or the most credible alternative reported by included studies.
- **Outcomes and timeframe:** Overdose survival and community overdose mortality.
- **Evidence summary/effect range:** Systematic reviews find high successful reversal and growing population-level evidence of mortality reduction, though delivery intensity varies.
- **Key studies:** [DRG-03](#drg-03)
- **Causal strength and bias:** Grade reflects the strongest applicable causal evidence and downgrades for bias, inconsistency, indirectness, or implementation variation.
- **U.S. applicability:** Direct U.S. evidence is prioritized; transfer across populations and delivery systems remains an explicit uncertainty.
- **Cost/resource evidence:** Not standardized in Phase 3. Record direct program cost, participant time, public-system use, price year, and perspective in Phase 4.
- **Implementation and fidelity:** Saturation coverage, free or low-cost supply, refills, hands-on training, calling emergency services, and outreach to high-risk transitions.
- **Scale, equity, and distribution:** Track reach, take-up, exclusion, racial/ethnic disparities, disability access, rural access, and burdens on participants—not only average effects.
- **Harms:** Inadequate supply, expired kits, failure to account for potent synthetic opioids, and inequitable distribution.
- **Recommended use:** People who use opioids, peers, families, shelters, libraries, and release or discharge settings.
- **Do not use as:** Do not limit distribution to people who can prove abstinence or treatment enrollment.
- **Primary metrics:** D-M1, D-M3
- **Open questions:** Optimal saturation thresholds; Rural and stimulant-contamination models

### D-I3: Comprehensive syringe services programs

**Evidence grade: Strong (high confidence).** Strong: based on the cited evidence, with no automatic transfer of this grade to a charity.

- **Definition and theory of change:** Provide sterile equipment, safe disposal, testing, vaccination, wound care, naloxone, and voluntary treatment linkage.
- **Target population and setting:** U.S. populations eligible for the intervention, with subgroup and local-system applicability checked before use.
- **Comparator:** Usual services, no intervention, or the most credible alternative reported by included studies.
- **Outcomes and timeframe:** HIV/HCV risk, injection practices, overdose response, treatment linkage, and community syringe disposal.
- **Evidence summary/effect range:** Convergent evidence and federal reviews support reduced infectious risk without evidence that SSPs increase drug use; comprehensive models provide broader benefits.
- **Key studies:** [DRG-06](#drg-06)
- **Causal strength and bias:** Grade reflects the strongest applicable causal evidence and downgrades for bias, inconsistency, indirectness, or implementation variation.
- **U.S. applicability:** Direct U.S. evidence is prioritized; transfer across populations and delivery systems remains an explicit uncertainty.
- **Cost/resource evidence:** Not standardized in Phase 3. Record direct program cost, participant time, public-system use, price year, and perspective in Phase 4.
- **Implementation and fidelity:** Adequate coverage, low-barrier hours, confidentiality, peer staff, disposal, testing, naloxone, and noncoercive treatment access.
- **Scale, equity, and distribution:** Track reach, take-up, exclusion, racial/ethnic disparities, disability access, rural access, and burdens on participants—not only average effects.
- **Harms:** Low coverage, police interference, stigma, and one-for-one restrictions can reduce effectiveness and safety.
- **Recommended use:** Communities with injection-related risk, designed with people who use drugs.
- **Do not use as:** Do not condition sterile equipment on treatment participation or abstinence.
- **Primary metrics:** D-M1, D-M3
- **Open questions:** Fentanyl and xylazine-era outcomes; Comparative delivery and coverage models

### D-I4: Contingency management for stimulant use disorder

**Evidence grade: Strong (moderate confidence).** Strong: based on the cited evidence, with no automatic transfer of this grade to a charity.

- **Definition and theory of change:** Provide predictable incentives contingent on objectively verified behavior such as stimulant-negative samples or attendance.
- **Target population and setting:** U.S. populations eligible for the intervention, with subgroup and local-system applicability checked before use.
- **Comparator:** Usual services, no intervention, or the most credible alternative reported by included studies.
- **Outcomes and timeframe:** Abstinence, attendance, and retention during treatment, with follow-up after incentives end.
- **Evidence summary/effect range:** A substantial trial literature supports contingency management as the most effective current behavioral intervention for stimulant use disorder, though durability varies.
- **Key studies:** [DRG-04](#drg-04)
- **Causal strength and bias:** Grade reflects the strongest applicable causal evidence and downgrades for bias, inconsistency, indirectness, or implementation variation.
- **U.S. applicability:** Direct U.S. evidence is prioritized; transfer across populations and delivery systems remains an explicit uncertainty.
- **Cost/resource evidence:** Not standardized in Phase 3. Record direct program cost, participant time, public-system use, price year, and perspective in Phase 4.
- **Implementation and fidelity:** Frequent testing, escalating incentives, immediate delivery, sufficient incentive value, anti-fraud controls, and respectful consent.
- **Scale, equity, and distribution:** Track reach, take-up, exclusion, racial/ethnic disparities, disability access, rural access, and burdens on participants—not only average effects.
- **Harms:** Underpowered incentives or delayed rewards reduce fidelity; punitive framing can undermine trust.
- **Recommended use:** People with stimulant use disorder who choose an incentive-based program.
- **Do not use as:** Do not redesign the incentive schedule so heavily that it no longer resembles tested models.
- **Primary metrics:** D-M2
- **Open questions:** Durability after incentives; Implementation under current payment rules; Effects on mortality

### D-I5: Overdose prevention / supervised consumption centers

**Evidence grade: Mixed (moderate confidence).** Mixed: credible evidence supports onsite safety and service linkage, while population-level overdose mortality findings are inconsistent and U.S. applicability is limited.

- **Definition and theory of change:** Provide a supervised setting where people consume pre-obtained drugs, receive rapid overdose response, and can connect to health and social services.
- **Target population and setting:** People who use drugs, especially people who otherwise use alone or in public, in U.S. communities where legally and operationally feasible.
- **Comparator:** No supervised facility, usual harm-reduction services, or areas and periods without access.
- **Outcomes and timeframe:** Onsite overdose survival, public drug use, service linkage, neighborhood outcomes, and population-level overdose mortality.
- **Evidence summary/effect range:** No reported onsite overdose deaths across long-running international services, but recent population-level mortality studies show mixed or null-to-protective associations depending on scale and coverage.
- **Key studies:** [DRG-07](#drg-07)
- **Causal strength and bias:** Onsite overdose response is directly observed, but population-level effects rely on a small heterogeneous observational and quasi-experimental evidence base.
- **U.S. applicability:** Most mature evidence is Canadian or international; U.S. legal context, fentanyl exposure, geography, and service saturation may change effects.
- **Cost/resource evidence:** Not standardized in Phase 3. Record facility, clinical and peer staffing, security, supplies, referral services, price year, and perspective in Phase 4.
- **Implementation and fidelity:** Accessible location and hours, trained responders, ventilation and infection control, naloxone and oxygen, noncoercive referral, participant privacy, and community engagement.
- **Scale, equity, and distribution:** Track who can reach and use the site, geographic coverage, policing around the site, disability access, race, housing status, and burdens or benefits to neighbors.
- **Harms:** Low coverage may leave population mortality unchanged; surveillance, policing, stigma, or exclusionary site rules can deter the highest-risk users.
- **Recommended use:** As a carefully evaluated component of a broader low-barrier harm-reduction system, with transparent coverage and neighborhood measures.
- **Do not use as:** Do not claim that one low-capacity site will measurably reduce citywide mortality or use police pressure to compel attendance.
- **Primary metrics:** D-M1, D-M3
- **Open questions:** Population mortality effects at adequate coverage; U.S. fentanyl-era outcomes; Effects of inhalation services and mobile models

### D-I6: Detoxification alone for opioid use disorder

**Evidence grade: Potentially Harmful (high confidence).** Potentially Harmful: based on the cited evidence, with no automatic transfer of this grade to a charity.

- **Definition and theory of change:** Provide short-term withdrawal management without ongoing medication treatment or an assured continuation plan.
- **Target population and setting:** U.S. populations eligible for the intervention, with subgroup and local-system applicability checked before use.
- **Comparator:** Usual services, no intervention, or the most credible alternative reported by included studies.
- **Outcomes and timeframe:** Return to opioid use, overdose, treatment entry, and mortality after discharge.
- **Evidence summary/effect range:** CDC guidance warns detoxification alone is not recommended because return to use and overdose risk increase after tolerance falls.
- **Key studies:** [DRG-02](#drg-02)
- **Causal strength and bias:** Grade reflects the strongest applicable causal evidence and downgrades for bias, inconsistency, indirectness, or implementation variation.
- **U.S. applicability:** Direct U.S. evidence is prioritized; transfer across populations and delivery systems remains an explicit uncertainty.
- **Cost/resource evidence:** Not standardized in Phase 3. Record direct program cost, participant time, public-system use, price year, and perspective in Phase 4.
- **Implementation and fidelity:** If withdrawal management occurs, initiate medication, provide naloxone, and arrange a confirmed continuing-care handoff.
- **Scale, equity, and distribution:** Track reach, take-up, exclusion, racial/ethnic disparities, disability access, rural access, and burdens on participants—not only average effects.
- **Harms:** Loss of tolerance followed by return to use can cause fatal overdose; discharge without care creates acute risk.
- **Recommended use:** Only as a bridge into ongoing evidence-based treatment chosen by the patient.
- **Do not use as:** Do not advertise completed detoxification as completion of OUD treatment.
- **Primary metrics:** D-M1, D-M2
- **Open questions:** Comparative transition models; Risk by detoxification setting

### D-I7: Compulsory or coercive drug detention/treatment

**Evidence grade: Potentially Harmful (moderate confidence).** Potentially Harmful: based on the cited evidence, with no automatic transfer of this grade to a charity.

- **Definition and theory of change:** Mandate treatment or confinement primarily because of drug use, without meaningful consent and individualized clinical/legal safeguards.
- **Target population and setting:** U.S. populations eligible for the intervention, with subgroup and local-system applicability checked before use.
- **Comparator:** Usual services, no intervention, or the most credible alternative reported by included studies.
- **Outcomes and timeframe:** Drug use, recidivism, mortality, rights violations, and treatment engagement after discharge.
- **Evidence summary/effect range:** A systematic review found no consistent benefit and some negative effects; coercive settings also present substantial rights and post-release risk.
- **Key studies:** [DRG-05](#drg-05)
- **Causal strength and bias:** Grade reflects the strongest applicable causal evidence and downgrades for bias, inconsistency, indirectness, or implementation variation.
- **U.S. applicability:** Direct U.S. evidence is prioritized; transfer across populations and delivery systems remains an explicit uncertainty.
- **Cost/resource evidence:** Not standardized in Phase 3. Record direct program cost, participant time, public-system use, price year, and perspective in Phase 4.
- **Implementation and fidelity:** Prioritize voluntary care; any legally mandated pathway requires due process, evidence-based treatment, clinical independence, and continuity.
- **Scale, equity, and distribution:** Track reach, take-up, exclusion, racial/ethnic disparities, disability access, rural access, and burdens on participants—not only average effects.
- **Harms:** Abuse, forced labor or confinement, loss of tolerance, disengagement, and increased recidivism in some studies.
- **Recommended use:** No recommended charitable use for compulsory detention models.
- **Do not use as:** Do not fund coercion as a substitute for accessible voluntary treatment.
- **Primary metrics:** D-M1, D-M2
- **Open questions:** U.S.-specific comparative studies; Post-release mortality and rights outcomes

## Research gaps and update triggers

### Priority research gaps

- Need fentanyl/polysubstance-era comparative evidence
- Mortality is rarely powered in behavioral trials
- Service reach and retention must accompany efficacy
- Harms and participant-defined wellbeing need routine measurement

### Early-update triggers

- Major systematic review or U.S. trial
- Material harm signal
- Policy or implementation change affecting applicability
- Scheduled review date

## Publication and interpretation guardrails

- Do not use to recommend a charity without a charity-specific applicability assessment.
- Do not compare grades as if they were cost-effectiveness scores.
- Separate participant wellbeing from government fiscal effects.
- Strong and Potentially Harmful grades require second review before public reliance.
