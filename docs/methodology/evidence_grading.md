# Evidence Grading Methodology

## Scope

Evidence grades summarize whether an **intervention** causes meaningful outcomes in relevant settings. They are not charity ratings. A charity-specific conclusion also requires implementation fidelity, population/context fit, cost, risk, and room for more funding.

## Review question

Before searching, specify population, intervention, comparator, outcomes, timeframe, geography, and adverse outcomes. Record protocol deviations. Prefer systematic searches and public research literature; document databases, terms, dates, inclusion criteria, exclusions, and duplicate handling.

## Evidence hierarchy—without mechanical scoring

Consider systematic reviews and meta-analyses, randomized trials, credible natural experiments and quasi-experimental studies, government evaluations, longitudinal comparisons, and implementation studies. Design labels are not enough: examine assignment, baseline balance, attrition, spillovers, outcome measurement, statistical power, selective reporting, model dependence, and conflicts of interest.

Self-reported pre/post change, participant satisfaction, reach, and outputs may inform implementation but usually do not identify causal impact.

## Required dimensions

Every grade memo addresses:

1. **Causal identification:** can observed differences reasonably be attributed to the intervention?
2. **Replication and consistency:** are effects reproduced across teams, sites, and specifications?
3. **U.S. relevance:** do institutions, populations, service systems, and baseline risks transfer to the target context?
4. **Magnitude and precision:** effect size, absolute baseline risk, interval estimates, and practical significance.
5. **Outcome importance and duration:** final wellbeing outcomes outrank short-lived proxies.
6. **Implementation difficulty:** staffing, fidelity, take-up, infrastructure, and monitoring demands.
7. **Scalability and external validity:** likely performance outside study conditions.
8. **Equity and distribution:** who benefits, who bears burdens, and whether averages conceal disparities.
9. **Risk of harm:** measured adverse effects, coercion, displacement, stigma, or plausible unmeasured harms.
10. **Cost evidence and freshness:** resource requirements, price year, perspective, and whether evidence remains current.

## Grade definitions

- **Strong:** multiple credible studies, preferably including independent replication, show meaningful beneficial effects on important outcomes in settings relevant to U.S. delivery; serious harms are unlikely to overturn the conclusion.
- **Moderate:** credible evidence supports benefit, but replication, context, duration, precision, or implementation limits remain material.
- **Promising:** early causal or unusually persuasive observational evidence is positive, but uncertainty is too high for a firmer conclusion.
- **Mixed:** credible studies point in materially different directions, important outcomes conflict, or benefits depend strongly on context.
- **Weak:** available evidence is predominantly low-credibility, imprecise, proxy-based, or fails to show meaningful benefit.
- **Potentially Harmful:** credible evidence indicates material adverse effects or a plausible harm large enough to dominate expected benefits in relevant use.
- **Insufficient Evidence:** too little applicable evidence exists to judge direction or magnitude.

“Insufficient” is not “ineffective,” and “weak” is not proof of zero effect. Grades do not convert automatically into numeric scores.

## Decision procedure

1. Complete the preregistered evidence-map template.
2. Build a study table including all eligible positive, null, and negative findings.
3. Rate risk of bias and applicability study by study.
4. Synthesize effects in natural units before translating them into common outcome units.
5. Draft the grade and the strongest case against it.
6. Obtain a second reviewer for `Strong`, `Potentially Harmful`, or recommendation-critical grades.
7. Resolve disagreements in writing; preserve minority views when unresolved.
8. Publish search date, evidence cut-off date, uncertainty, and next review date.

## Charity applicability

A charity does not receive an intervention’s grade automatically. The charity review must test whether its target population, dosage, staff qualifications, fidelity, complementary services, outcome definitions, and local systems match the evidence. Applicability can be `high`, `moderate`, `low`, or `unknown`, with reasons.

## Updates

Reassess when a major new review or trial appears, a material harm signal emerges, implementation changes, or the scheduled review date arrives. Top recommendations require at least annual review; stale grades must be visibly marked.
