Intervention Type

Example Reentry Initiative delivers a combined transitional employment and housing support intervention targeting people in the first year after release from a state or federal correctional facility. The model has two linked components. The employment component places participants in paid transitional jobs with partnered employers — typically in construction, logistics, food service, and light manufacturing — while providing on-the-job coaching, conflict mediation, and financial literacy training. The housing component provides temporary rental subsidies for up to six months combined with case management that assists participants in finding and retaining permanent affordable housing.

The two components are integrated rather than independent: participants must maintain both housing stability and employment engagement to remain enrolled, and case managers coordinate across both domains. This bundled approach reflects research indicating that employment instability and housing instability are mutually reinforcing barriers during reentry, and that addressing only one without the other produces smaller, less durable outcomes.

The program does not require sobriety, mental health treatment enrollment, or completion of prior programming as a condition of participation. This low-barrier entry design follows evidence from Housing First homelessness research and is increasingly adopted by reentry programs with strong outcome data.

Evidence Assessment
Strong Evidence Based on intervention model · not org-specific research

The evidence grade reflects the strength of the research base for the transitional employment plus housing support model as an intervention type, not ClearView-conducted research on this specific organization. When evaluating organizations, ClearView distinguishes between (a) whether the intervention model they use is backed by rigorous evidence, and (b) whether this specific organization delivers it with fidelity and adequate quality. Both questions matter; only the first can currently be answered for this placeholder.

The transitional employment model for reentry has been evaluated in multiple randomized controlled trials in the United States since the 1980s. The most rigorous studies — conducted by MDRC and independent research teams in New York, Chicago, and Ohio — find consistent reductions in reincarceration rates of 10–20 percentage points over 12–24 month follow-up periods for participants randomized to transitional employment compared to control conditions. Effect sizes are larger for individuals with longer prior incarceration histories and limited formal work histories. Studies vary in whether employment earnings effects persist beyond the program period; some show fade-out while others show durable labor market gains.

Key studies informing this grade (cited by type, not as ClearView-verified sources for this organization): multiple RCTs of Center for Employment Opportunities and comparable transitional jobs programs; MDRC's Building Tomorrow's Workforce study; quasi-experimental evaluations of bundled reentry programs in Illinois and Texas using matched administrative records; a meta-analysis of 42 reentry intervention studies published in a major criminology journal.

Important uncertainty notes: (1) Most RCTs were conducted with voluntary participants who may differ from those served by programs without the same selection processes. (2) Follow-up periods rarely exceed 36 months; longer-run recidivism and employment effects are unknown. (3) Effect sizes vary across sites and populations — a national organization operating across many contexts may average substantially different results than the best-performing trial sites. (4) No independent evaluation of Example Reentry Initiative specifically has been reviewed because this is a placeholder page.

This cost-effectiveness estimate was derived using the following placeholder methodology: (1) program cost per participant drawn from representative transitional reentry programs in published literature; (2) recidivism reduction effect size from the central estimate of RCT meta-analysis; (3) baseline recidivism rate of approximately 40% at two years post-release, drawn from Bureau of Justice Statistics national data; (4) adjustment for partial program compliance and attrition. The figure represents the cost of producing one avoided reincarceration event, valued at the human welfare benefit to the individual — not the fiscal savings to government, which are reported separately and are not counted twice.

Separate fiscal savings estimate: avoided incarceration costs to state and federal governments are estimated at $30,000–$40,000 per person-year of avoided incarceration. These fiscal returns significantly exceed program costs in expectation. However, ClearView does not lead with fiscal savings in recommendations because (a) they accrue to government, not to donors, (b) they depend on whether system capacity is genuinely reduced or merely shifted, and (c) the causal chain from avoided rearrest to avoided incarceration is not 1:1.

Note on false precision: The figures above are illustrative model outputs, not the result of real data collection. Real cost-effectiveness estimates require: verified program cost per participant from audited financials, confirmed participant enrollment counts, independent outcome data, and sensitivity analysis across key assumptions. ClearView will publish full calculation worksheets with all assumptions when real reviews are conducted. The range shown here ($8,000–$14,000) is deliberately wide to reflect genuine uncertainty; collapsing it to a single number would misrepresent what is known.
