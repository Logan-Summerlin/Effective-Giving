Evidence Summary

The recidivism reduction literature is one of the more mature bodies of evidence in domestic social policy, with hundreds of evaluations conducted since the 1970s. However, the quality of that evidence is uneven. Early studies relied heavily on pre-post comparisons and administrative records with no comparison group, making it difficult to separate program effects from changes in supervision intensity or criminal justice policy. The most credible recent studies use randomized controlled trials, regression discontinuity designs exploiting sentencing thresholds, or propensity-score matched comparison groups. ClearView will weight causal identification strength heavily when rating interventions and individual programs.

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy has the strongest and most consistent evidence base of any reentry intervention. CBT programs targeting criminal thinking patterns — such as antisocial attitudes, impulsivity, and poor problem-solving — reduce recidivism by roughly 10–30% relative to comparison groups in well-designed studies. The effect is fairly robust across different CBT variants, delivery settings (prison vs. community), and participant demographics. Importantly, CBT is also among the least expensive interventions per participant, making the cost-effectiveness profile attractive relative to other approaches. The main limitation is that high-fidelity implementation requires trained facilitators, and program drift in community settings can attenuate effects.

Transitional employment programs, which provide paid subsidized work immediately after release, show strong short-term reductions in rearrest in multiple RCTs — most notably the Center for Employment Opportunities (CEO) studies. The mechanism appears to involve both income replacement during the high-risk period immediately post-release and the structured daily routine that employment provides. However, several evaluations show that employment effects fade after the transitional period ends unless participants are connected to unsubsidized jobs. Program effectiveness is sensitive to local labor market conditions and the quality of job development and retention services.

Prison education is associated with large reductions in recidivism in observational studies, but establishing causal identification is difficult because more motivated individuals self-select into programs. The most credible analysis, a 2013 RAND meta-analysis of correctional education, found that participants were 43% less likely to return to prison than non-participants and were more likely to be employed post-release, with favorable cost-benefit ratios even under conservative assumptions. However, several studies rely on intent-to-treat designs compromised by limited program capacity, and the counterfactual group sometimes includes people who could not access programs due to capacity constraints rather than lack of interest. More recent evaluations using instrumental variable approaches have found smaller but still positive effects.

The most significant challenge for cost-effectiveness analysis in this cause area is that recidivism is a system-level outcome influenced by policing intensity, prosecutorial discretion, and supervision conditions — all of which are largely outside the control of nonprofit reentry organizations. A charity operating an excellent transitional employment program in a jurisdiction that has recently increased surveillance of people on parole may show worse rearrest outcomes than a mediocre program operating in a lower-supervision context. ClearView will note these contextual factors in evaluations and will avoid penalizing charities for systemic factors outside their control.

Key Metrics We Track
Cost per Rearrest Avoided

Total program cost divided by the estimated reduction in rearrest events relative to a comparable non-participant population, measured at 12 and 36 months post-release. This requires a credible comparison group and administrative data linkage. We will not accept self-reported outcomes as the primary metric for this indicator.

Cost per Reincarceration Avoided

A stricter version of the rearrest metric that counts only episodes resulting in a new prison or jail commitment. Reincarceration is a higher-specificity outcome than rearrest since it is less sensitive to changes in local policing patterns, though it is also less frequent, which creates statistical power challenges for smaller programs.

Sustained Employment Rate

The share of participants employed in unsubsidized jobs at 6 and 12 months post-program exit. We distinguish between subsidized transitional employment — which is the program itself — and unsubsidized placement, which is the durable outcome. Programs that count transitional job hours as employment outcomes without tracking unsubsidized placement will be noted.

Housing Stability Rate

The share of participants in stable, non-shelter housing at 6 and 12 months after release or program exit. Housing instability is a major proximate cause of reincarceration, and tracking it separately allows ClearView to evaluate whether programs are addressing the social determinants of recidivism in addition to criminogenic thinking patterns.

Research Gaps

Despite a large volume of published evaluations, several fundamental questions about reentry intervention effectiveness remain unresolved and will shape how ClearView rates individual charities.

Long-run effects beyond two years. Most rigorous evaluations track participants for 12–24 months post-release, a period during which supervision conditions and program supports are typically still active. Evidence on whether recidivism reductions persist at five and ten years — after supervision ends and program contact fades — is sparse. Some early employment studies found that treatment-group advantages diminished or disappeared in multi-year follow-up, while CBT studies have shown more durable effects. Understanding what drives differential durability across intervention types is critical for cost-effectiveness analysis but requires longitudinal administrative data linkages that many organizations cannot access.

Heterogeneous treatment effects by risk level. The risk-needs-responsivity (RNR) framework, the dominant theoretical model in reentry practice, predicts that intensive interventions produce the greatest reductions when targeted to high-risk individuals and matched to their specific criminogenic needs. However, many published evaluations test programs on broad or mixed-risk samples without stratifying results by baseline risk score. As a result, it is often unclear whether a moderate average treatment effect reflects a large effect for high-risk individuals and a near-zero effect for low-risk individuals — a pattern that would dramatically change cost-effectiveness calculations, since low-risk individuals may be harmed by intensive programs through exposure to higher-risk peers.

Role of housing and community conditions. Reentry research has historically focused on individual-level interventions (skills, employment, treatment) and underinvested in understanding how neighborhood-level conditions — housing affordability, concentrated poverty, neighborhood social capital — moderate individual program effects. A transitional employment program may perform well in a context with a tight labor market and affordable housing and poorly in a context of high unemployment and housing scarcity, even holding program fidelity constant. Building this contextual variation into cost-effectiveness estimates requires multi-site studies with variation in community conditions, which are rare and expensive.
