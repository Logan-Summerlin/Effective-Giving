Legal Immigrant Integration

Naturalization assistance, legal aid, English education, credential recognition, and family stability services for people who are legally present in the U.S.

Important Legal Category Distinctions: "Immigrants" and "immigrants in need of services" are not monolithic categories. People who are legally present in the U.S. face very different legal processes and service needs depending on their status. Refugees are admitted through federal resettlement programs with immediate work authorization and a defined path to permanent residency. Asylum seekers have applied for but not yet received protection and face significant work-authorization delays. Lawful Permanent Residents (LPRs / green card holders) have work authorization and residency rights but must wait years before naturalizing. TPS (Temporary Protected Status) holders have work authorization but no clear path to permanent status. Visa holders (employment, student, humanitarian parole, etc.) have highly varied rights and timelines. Naturalization applicants are LPRs seeking citizenship. Programs and charities that are effective for one group may be irrelevant or inapplicable to another. ClearView will clearly specify which legal category a program serves in all evaluations.

Evidence Summary

The immigrant integration evidence base is considerably smaller and more fragmented than the literature on homelessness or recidivism, in part because research on immigrant outcomes has historically been conducted primarily by economists studying population-level assimilation trends rather than by program evaluators studying specific nonprofit interventions. As a result, there is strong evidence about what integration outcomes look like at scale — wage convergence trajectories, naturalization rates by country of origin, language acquisition patterns — but far less rigorous evidence on which specific programs or services cause better individual-level outcomes. ClearView will be transparent about this gap and will weight existing evidence accordingly.

Naturalization is one of the most well-studied outcomes in the immigrant integration literature, in part because it is a discrete, verifiable legal event with large documented effects on earnings, civic participation, and long-term stability. Research using naturalization eligibility thresholds finds that citizenship confers an earnings premium of roughly 5–10% net of selection effects, driven largely by improved job search and access to public-sector employment. Programs that help LPRs navigate the naturalization process address a real barrier: the application is complex, requires English proficiency certification, and carries a filing fee of several hundred dollars. Organizations that provide fee waivers, exam preparation, and legal review are addressing the most binding constraints for low-income LPRs, and the per-citizenship cost of these interventions is relatively low given the scale of the earnings benefit.

Legal representation in immigration proceedings has a documented and dramatic effect on case outcomes. Studies of immigration court data consistently find that represented respondents are several times more likely to receive relief than unrepresented respondents with similar case characteristics. The effect is particularly pronounced in asylum cases, where unrepresented applicants face approval rates of roughly 20% versus 45–60% for represented applicants in some analyses. However, measuring the charitable contribution of legal aid organizations requires attention to the counterfactual — what representation would the person have received without the organization? In jurisdictions with robust public legal aid systems, the additionality of nonprofit organizations may be smaller than in under-served regions. ClearView will assess local legal aid availability when evaluating individual organizations.

English language acquisition is consistently associated with better economic outcomes in observational research, with each additional English proficiency level associated with meaningful wage gains. However, establishing the causal effect of specific ESL programs — rather than the selection effect of motivated learners — is methodologically difficult, and few rigorous RCTs of adult ESL programs exist. The most credible evidence comes from workplace ESL programs, where random assignment within employer cohorts is sometimes feasible, and from refugee resettlement contexts where administrative data can track earnings trajectories. Community ESL programs, which serve the largest number of people, have the weakest causal evidence despite being the most common target of philanthropic funding.

The refugee resettlement system offers one of the better long-run datasets on integration, with USCIS and academic researchers tracking earnings, employment, and benefit use across cohorts over decades. Research by economists including Kalena Cortes and William Evans finds that refugees who arrived in stronger local economies and were placed by resettlement agencies with more resources showed significantly better long-run outcomes, providing suggestive evidence that resettlement quality matters. Notably, refugees placed in smaller cities with tighter social networks and lower initial competition for entry-level jobs showed stronger earnings trajectories than those placed in large coastal metros — a finding with implications for which resettlement organizations ClearView might rate most favorably.

Key Metrics We Track
Cost per Naturalization

Total program cost for naturalization assistance programs divided by the number of successful citizenship approvals, net of the rate that applicants would have succeeded without assistance. Because many LPRs who seek help would eventually naturalize without it, attribution requires comparison with an eligible non-participant group or an estimate of the program's incremental success rate above the baseline.

Cost per Work Authorization Secured

For legal aid programs serving individuals without current work authorization — including asylum seekers, TPS holders, and individuals with pending status adjustments — we will track cost per successful work permit obtained. Work authorization is a critical gateway to economic independence and is often the most immediate and verifiable outcome of legal representation.

Earnings Gain

The most consequential long-run outcome for integration programs is earnings. Where administrative wage data linkages are available, ClearView will estimate earnings gains attributable to the intervention — naturalization, credential recognition, or job placement — relative to comparable individuals who did not receive services. We will distinguish earnings gains from reclassification (e.g., getting a license to work in a field one was already working in informally) from genuine human capital or opportunity gains.

Long-Term Family Stability

Integration outcomes extend beyond individual economics to family-level stability: are households maintaining housing, keeping children in school, and avoiding displacement or deportation proceedings? For programs providing family stability services, we will track housing retention, children's school enrollment, and absence of new immigration enforcement contact at 12 and 24 months, recognizing that these are harder to attribute to any single program.

Research Gaps

The immigrant integration evidence base has important gaps that will shape how ClearView approaches charity evaluation in this cause area, and where ClearView will need to develop new analytical methods rather than relying on existing literature.

Long-run integration trajectories for non-refugee pathways. The best long-run integration data comes from refugee cohorts, who are tracked by federal agencies and have been studied by economists with access to administrative records. Comparable long-run data on LPRs, TPS holders, and other non-refugee pathways is scarcer, and most existing studies rely on Census survey data rather than administrative wage and tax records. As a result, the long-run earnings trajectory for people who naturalize or receive legal aid through nonprofit programs — and therefore the full benefit of these interventions — is not well-documented. ClearView will work with organizations that have administrative data partnerships and will flag the limitations of studies relying solely on self-reported survey outcomes.

Heterogeneous effects by country of origin, language, and legal status. Integration trajectories differ dramatically by country of origin, pre-migration education, language distance from English, and legal status pathway. A program that shows strong average outcomes may perform very differently for recently arrived Vietnamese-speaking elders, college-educated Indian tech workers, or Central American TPS holders — populations that may all be classified as "legal immigrants" but face completely different integration challenges. ClearView will require that evaluations specify the target population and will be skeptical of average effects drawn from highly heterogeneous samples without subgroup analysis.

Policy environment sensitivity. Integration program outcomes are highly sensitive to federal immigration policy, which has changed substantially across administrations and will continue to change. Naturalization timelines, work authorization processing speeds, TPS designation status, and enforcement priorities all affect the environment in which nonprofits operate and the outcomes they can achieve. Programs that performed well under one policy environment may be more or less effective under changed conditions. ClearView will note the policy environment at the time of any evaluation and will flag studies where results may not generalize to the current policy context. This is a particular concern for evaluations of programs serving asylum seekers and TPS holders, whose legal pathways have been subject to significant policy volatility.
