Drug Addiction & Harm Reduction

Medication-assisted treatment, harm reduction, naloxone distribution, overdose prevention, and recovery support.

Note on political controversy: Several evidence-based harm reduction programs — including syringe service programs, naloxone distribution, and overdose prevention centers — are politically controversial in some jurisdictions. ClearView evaluates charities and interventions on the strength of scientific evidence for reducing death and disease, not on alignment with any particular moral, political, or ideological position. Programs with strong evidence of saving lives will be rated accordingly, regardless of controversy. Where genuine scientific uncertainty exists, we will say so clearly. Where political opposition exists but scientific consensus does not, we will also say so clearly.

Syringe Service Programs (SSPs) Strong Placeholder Cost per HIV/HCV infection avoided CDC and SAMHSA endorse SSPs as evidence-based. Multiple systematic reviews find they reduce HIV and hepatitis C transmission by 50% or more among people who inject drugs, with no evidence of increased drug use in the community. SSPs also serve as a gateway to treatment; clients who access SSPs are more likely to enter treatment than those who do not.
Contingency Management Strong Placeholder Cost per sustained abstinence outcome Contingency management — providing tangible rewards (gift cards, vouchers) for drug-free urine tests — has the strongest evidence base of any behavioral treatment for stimulant use disorder (methamphetamine, cocaine), for which no approved medication exists. The VA and SAMHSA have both endorsed it; implementation has been limited by regulatory barriers that have recently eased.
Recovery Housing Moderate Placeholder Cost per sustained recovery outcome Sober living environments and recovery homes provide substance-free housing and peer support for individuals in early recovery. Several studies find associations with reduced relapse and improved employment; causal identification is difficult due to selection. Oxford Houses have been studied more rigorously than most recovery housing models.
Peer Navigation Promising Placeholder Cost per person linked to treatment Peer recovery coaches — people with lived experience of addiction — support engagement in treatment, navigation of services, and maintenance of recovery. Several RCTs and quasi-experimental studies find that peer navigation increases treatment initiation and retention rates; effect sizes are modest but costs are also low relative to clinical interventions.
Overdose Prevention Centers (OPCs) Promising Placeholder Cost per overdose death averted Supervised consumption sites have operated for decades in Canada, Europe, and Australia with no reported on-site overdose deaths and strong evidence of community-level mortality reduction. Two officially sanctioned OPCs now operate in New York City. U.S.-specific evidence is nascent; international evidence is strong. Political opposition limits availability.
Evidence Summary

Drug addiction treatment and harm reduction represent one of the highest-evidence cause areas in domestic social policy. Medication-assisted treatment with buprenorphine or methadone and naloxone distribution for overdose reversal rank among the most rigorously proven interventions in all of medicine — not just social policy. The evidence base includes hundreds of randomized controlled trials, multiple Cochrane systematic reviews, and decades of real-world implementation data from multiple countries. Despite this overwhelming evidence, access to effective treatment remains severely limited in the United States: an estimated 80% of people with opioid use disorder do not receive any evidence-based treatment, and treatment options in rural areas and jails/prisons are often nonexistent. This treatment access gap is the primary lever available to philanthropists in this cause area.

Medication-assisted treatment — particularly buprenorphine (Suboxone) and methadone maintenance — has demonstrated in multiple large RCTs and observational studies that it reduces overdose mortality by approximately 50% relative to no treatment or abstinence-based treatment alone. Buprenorphine can be prescribed in office-based settings (following the 2023 removal of the X-waiver requirement for buprenorphine prescribing, access has expanded) and via telehealth, dramatically increasing potential reach. Methadone for opioid use disorder remains restricted to federally certified opioid treatment programs, creating access barriers in regions without clinic capacity. Programs that train providers, reduce administrative barriers, and directly prescribe buprenorphine are addressing the highest-evidence treatment approach at a point where marginal funding can expand access significantly.

Naloxone distribution is equally well-supported by the evidence base. Naloxone rapidly reverses opioid overdoses and, administered promptly, prevents overdose death with high reliability. Community distribution programs — which place naloxone and training with people who use drugs, family members, and community members — have been shown to reduce overdose mortality at the community level in studies from Massachusetts, Baltimore, and multiple other sites. The per-dose cost of naloxone has fallen substantially following generic entry, and generic intranasal formulations are now widely available. Organizations that focus on community distribution in high-burden areas, co-prescription alongside opioid prescribing, and jail-to-community naloxone handoff programs represent high-value targets for philanthropy given low cost per unit and high evidence of benefit.

Syringe service programs have been endorsed by the CDC, the Surgeon General, and every major public health body as evidence-based. They reduce HIV transmission by over 50% among people who inject drugs; reduce hepatitis C transmission substantially; serve as a gateway to treatment engagement; and do not increase drug use in the community — a claim that has been tested in multiple studies across multiple countries. Despite this, SSPs face significant legal and political barriers in many U.S. states, with a number of states still banning the use of public funds for needles. Nonprofits operating SSPs in these environments are providing a genuinely irreplaceable service that public health systems cannot deliver. ClearView will evaluate SSPs on public health outcomes (HIV/HCV infections avoided) rather than abstinence-focused metrics, which are not the primary intended outcome of harm reduction programs.

The addiction cause area also illustrates the importance of separating evidence quality from political controversy in charity evaluation. Contingency management — one of the strongest behavioral interventions for stimulant use disorder — was prohibited in the VA's telehealth platform for years due to federal gift rules, even though it has extensive RCT support. Overdose prevention centers have decades of international evidence with zero recorded on-site deaths at any site globally, yet only opened in the U.S. in 2021. Abstinence-only treatment programs — which are politically popular in some quarters — have substantially weaker evidence of preventing overdose mortality than medication-assisted approaches. ClearView will rate interventions based on the quality and quantity of evidence for reducing harm, not on political palatability.

Key Metrics We Track
Cost per Overdose Death Averted

For MAT access, naloxone distribution, and overdose prevention center programs, we will estimate deaths averted relative to a counterfactual without the program. This requires community-level overdose mortality data and a credible estimate of the program's contribution. We will use conservative assumptions and will report uncertainty ranges explicitly rather than presenting single point estimates.

Cost per Nonfatal Overdose Avoided

For programs where mortality data is unavailable or where the program primarily prevents nonfatal overdoses, we will estimate the number of overdose reversals attributable to naloxone distribution or on-site supervision, converted to a per-dollar figure. We will note that nonfatal overdose prevention has value both intrinsically and as a pathway to treatment engagement.

Cost per Person Retained in Treatment

For MAT and recovery support programs, treatment retention at 6 and 12 months is the primary process metric predictive of mortality reduction. We will calculate cost per person remaining engaged in evidence-based treatment at these time points, using administrative data where available and survey-based follow-up otherwise. We will clearly note the evidence supporting retention as a proxy for mortality outcomes.

Cost per QALY Gained

For programs with sufficient outcome data, we will attempt to calculate cost per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) gained using standard health economics methods. MAT for opioid use disorder has been shown in multiple analyses to fall well below accepted cost-effectiveness thresholds used in healthcare. We will use published utility weights from the addiction literature and will expose all assumptions in our calculations.

Research Gaps

Despite the unusually strong evidence base for several interventions in this cause area, meaningful gaps remain — particularly around implementation in new contexts, newer drug trends, and the effectiveness of nonprofit organizations specifically relative to publicly funded systems.

Fentanyl-era effectiveness of existing interventions. The U.S. overdose crisis shifted dramatically around 2016 with the proliferation of illicit fentanyl, which is roughly 50 times more potent than heroin, is often present in counterfeit pills and non-opioid drug supplies, and requires faster naloxone administration and sometimes multiple doses to reverse. The evidence base for most harm reduction interventions — naloxone dosing, SSP protocols, outreach approaches — was developed in an era of primarily heroin use. While the core interventions remain valid and beneficial, their optimal delivery parameters may differ in a fentanyl-saturated environment, and some research suggests that naloxone-to-overdose ratios have shifted. Programs that have adapted protocols for fentanyl (distributing higher-dose naloxone, providing fentanyl test strips, adjusting overdose response training) may be more effective than programs using legacy models, but direct comparisons are limited.

Methamphetamine and polysubstance use disorder. While the opioid crisis has dominated public and philanthropic attention, stimulant use disorder — particularly methamphetamine — now accounts for a growing share of overdose deaths (often in combination with opioids) and presents a distinct treatment challenge since no FDA-approved medication for methamphetamine use disorder exists. Contingency management is the only intervention with strong evidence for stimulant use disorder, but it has been hampered by regulatory barriers and limited uptake in nonprofit settings. The gap between evidence and practice is particularly stark for methamphetamine: effective nonprofit programs for this population are rare, and donors seeking to fund the highest-evidence interventions for stimulant use disorder have limited options relative to opioid programs. ClearView will flag this gap explicitly.

Rural and carceral access gaps. The strongest evidence for MAT comes from urban treatment programs with relatively good access to pharmacies, clinical infrastructure, and case management. People in rural areas and people incarcerated in county jails — who face among the highest overdose mortality rates in the immediate post-release period — represent populations where access to evidence-based treatment is most limited but evidence on how best to deliver it is also thinnest. Telehealth buprenorphine prescribing is a promising approach for rural access, but evidence on long-term retention via telehealth versus in-person care is still accumulating. Jail-based MAT programs have strong pilot evidence but remain rare nationally; organizations working to scale this model in jails and prisons address a genuine evidence-backed gap, though organizational capacity and county-level politics make evaluation difficult.
