Evidence Summary

The homelessness intervention space has a stronger evidence base than most domestic social-policy cause areas, largely because of sustained government and philanthropic investment in rigorous evaluation over the past three decades. The HUD-McKinney-Vento research portfolio and the U.S. Interagency Council on Homelessness have funded numerous randomized and quasi-experimental studies, particularly around Permanent Supportive Housing and Rapid Rehousing.

Permanent Supportive Housing has the strongest evidence of any homelessness intervention. Multiple RCTs, including the landmark Pathways to Housing studies beginning in the 1990s, demonstrate that Housing First models — which provide stable housing without sobriety or treatment preconditions — consistently achieve higher rates of stable housing than treatment-first approaches. The evidence holds across populations, including people with severe mental illness and co-occurring substance use disorders. However, the fiscal savings often cited in advocacy materials (reduced ER visits, incarceration, etc.) are real but smaller in more recent rigorous studies than in earlier cost-benefit analyses, which sometimes double-counted or used high-end estimates.

Rapid Rehousing has strong evidence for families experiencing homelessness, where most episodes are caused by acute economic crises rather than chronic barriers. For individuals — particularly those with untreated mental illness or long histories of street homelessness — RRH exit rates and housing stability are lower, and the evidence is more mixed. The most credible studies use comparison groups with similar needs-assessment scores to control for selection.

Prevention and diversion programs present a challenging attribution problem: because most households who receive emergency rental assistance would have resolved their housing crisis without intervention, per-dollar impact estimates are highly sensitive to the assumed counterfactual. Programs that use strong targeting criteria (income-verified, court-verified eviction threat) have better evidence of incremental effect than broadly accessible rental assistance funds.

The weakest area of evidence is around emergency shelter operating as a standalone intervention. Shelter reduces immediate risk of harm but has not been shown to produce durable housing outcomes without active connection to permanent housing pathways. Cost comparisons between shelter and rapid rehousing sometimes overlook the higher needs of individuals who end up in shelter for extended periods. ClearView will weight evidence quality, causal identification strength, and U.S. applicability in all ratings.

Key Metrics We Track
Cost per Person Housed

Total program cost divided by the number of individuals or households placed in stable housing, including direct assistance, case management, and pro-rated administrative overhead. ClearView will compare this across charities within the same intervention type, not across intervention types, since PSH and RRH serve populations with different needs.

Cost per Year of Housing Stability

A more demanding metric that accounts for how long people remain housed after placement. A cheaper placement that results in rapid return to homelessness scores worse on this metric than a costlier placement with multi-year stability. We will use 12-month and 24-month follow-up data where available.

Shelter-Night Reduction

For programs operating in systems with good administrative data, we will estimate the reduction in emergency shelter bed-nights attributable to the program. This allows a comparison between upstream prevention costs and downstream shelter costs, though causality is difficult to establish at the program level.

Emergency Service Cost Offset

Fiscal savings from reduced emergency department visits, inpatient hospitalizations, and incarceration spells attributable to stable housing. We will report this separately from human welfare benefits and apply conservative assumptions, since the best recent studies show smaller savings than earlier advocacy-driven estimates.

Research Gaps

Despite the relatively strong evidence base in this cause area, several important questions remain unresolved and will affect how ClearView rates individual charities.

Long-run housing stability beyond two years. Most RCTs and evaluations track outcomes for 12–24 months post-placement. There is limited evidence on whether housing gains persist over 5–10 year time horizons, or whether ongoing case management support is required to maintain stability. Programs that achieve high short-term housing rates but have high long-term return-to-homelessness rates may be less cost-effective than they appear.

Unsheltered vs. sheltered populations. Most strong evidence comes from individuals who are already in contact with shelter systems. Evidence on programs reaching the persistently unsheltered — those avoiding shelter due to barriers, trauma, or distrust — is thinner, and cost-per-outcome estimates in this subpopulation may be substantially higher.

Rural homelessness. The homelessness evidence base is overwhelmingly urban. Rural homelessness is mostly hidden — overcrowded housing, staying with relatives, living in vehicles — and programs designed for urban shelter-to-housing pipelines may transfer poorly to rural contexts. ClearView will note this limitation when evaluating organizations operating in rural areas.

Prevention targeting efficiency. Knowing which households would have become homeless without assistance remains a fundamental challenge for prevention programs. The counterfactual is unobservable at the individual level. The best programs use high-specificity targeting criteria validated against administrative data, but this methodology is not universal, and the field lacks consensus on optimal eligibility thresholds.

Cash transfer evidence maturity. Programs like the Denver Basic Income Project and various guaranteed income pilots provide promising early results for housing stability, but the evidence base is young, sample sizes are modest, and selection into pilots may not represent typical service populations. ClearView will monitor this literature and update ratings as more findings become available.
